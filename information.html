Section 1: Regional & PEST Analysis

Southern and south-eastern Kazakhstan, including Turkistan Region, Zhambyl Region, Kyzylorda Region, Almaty Region, and Jetisu Region, form a combined market of approximately 6.5 million people, or 31.6% of Kazakhstan's total population. These regions are characterized by demographic growth, infrastructure expansion, industrial modernization, increasing urbanization, and rising demand for qualified specialists.

Regional development priorities include agriculture modernization, logistics and transportation, industrial production, entrepreneurship, healthcare, digitalization, and infrastructure development. This creates a practical basis for an applied university model that connects information technology with sector-specific training.

Economic and sectoral context

Agricultural output in January-April 2026 reached 202.5 billion KZT in Turkistan Region, 159.8 billion KZT in Almaty Region, 65.1 billion KZT in Zhambyl Region, 56.0 billion KZT in Jetisu Region, and 22.1 billion KZT in Kyzylorda Region. Industrial production ranges from 142.8 billion KZT in Jetisu Region to more than 804.8 billion KZT in Almaty Region. These indicators point to increasing demand for specialists who combine IT, applied engineering skills, and industry-specific knowledge.

Labor market demand

Key sectors with growing labor demand include AgriTech, logistics and supply chain management, manufacturing, construction, finance, healthcare, education, and digital services. Kazakhstan also faces an annual shortage of approximately 30,000 ICT specialists. ICT-related programs are already among the most popular fields of study: in 2025, 21% of graduates selected engineering and ICT-related specialties.

Education capacity and regional access

Technical and vocational education enrollment has grown significantly in the target regions, especially in Turkistan, Zhambyl, and Almaty regions. This demonstrates demand for practical, employment-oriented, and industry-connected education. At the same time, many higher education opportunities remain concentrated in Almaty and other major cities, contributing to educational migration and unequal access for students from southern regions.

Strategic alignment with Alatau

According to the Alatau master plan, the Golden District is planned as an international educational and medical cluster designed for up to 40,000 students. The AU model aligns with this development logic through an IT major combined with minors in Agriculture, Economics, Health, and Education, corresponding to AgriTech, FinTech and Business Systems, HealthTech, and EdTech.

Institutional rationale

The creation of an applied IT university in Alatau responds directly to regional labor market demand and development strategies. It supports economic diversification, digital transformation, workforce development, and modernization of key industries. The proposed model is therefore not only an educational initiative, but also a regional development instrument for southern and south-eastern Kazakhstan.

Section 2

Marketing & Funnel Strategy

Alatau University's enrollment strategy is structured around two principal audiences: high school students in Grades 10-11 and their parents. The primary geographic focus includes Taraz, Shymkent, Turkistan, Kyzylorda, and the wider Southern Kazakhstan region, with later expansion toward neighboring Central Asian markets.

The student audience is addressed through themes of information technology, artificial intelligence, English-medium education, scholarships, international mobility, and career prospects. The parent audience is addressed through employment outcomes, campus safety, discipline, affordability, dormitory quality, and the long-term value of an English-language technology education.

Awareness and regional outreach

The first-year awareness objective is to reach at least 100,000 students and parents through a structured regional outreach program. The proposed AI Future Tour is an offline roadshow across Taraz, Shymkent, Turkistan, Kyzylorda, and Almaty under the academic theme "Future Professions & Artificial Intelligence." Its purpose is to introduce the university's educational model, explain emerging professions, and build trust in regions where students often feel compelled to move to larger cities for technology education.

Digital communication strategy

The digital strategy separates channels by function. TikTok is treated as a visibility channel, with content on future careers, student lifestyle, transformation stories, and parent-focused topics such as safety and employment. Instagram is positioned as a trust and brand-quality channel, emphasizing campus visuals, faculty profiles, scholarship announcements, laboratory demonstrations, and international partnerships. YouTube is used for longer-form authority building, including documentary-style videos, interviews with founders, industry talks, and accessible explanations of artificial intelligence.

Lead generation and conversion

Lead generation is built around educational value rather than direct promotion. Proposed lead magnets include a free AI career test, IELTS/SAT/ENT mock exams, and an AI Olympiad that can function both as a scholarship pipeline and a public academic event. Conversion activities are centered on Campus Experience Days, where prospective students participate in robotics workshops, coding challenges, AI demonstrations, startup simulations, and English-speaking activities.

Partnerships, public relations, and community development

The marketing model is supported by partnerships with technology companies, banks, EdTech startups, agrotech companies, hospitals, and AI startups. Public relations should emphasize institutional positioning rather than promotional claims: the emergence of an AI-focused university in Southern Kazakhstan, Taraz as a developing technology hub, and the integration of AI into student work from the first year. Community-building mechanisms include AI clubs in schools, coding competitions, hackathons, conferences, and a student ambassador program with scholarships, merchandise, networking access, and internship opportunities.

Section 4

Organizational Structure

University Leadership

Executive leadership responsible for institutional strategy, partnerships, academic quality, and operational excellence.

Board of Directors

Governance body supporting long-term strategy, investment priorities, and institutional accountability.

Academic Councils

Cross-school academic governance for curriculum, research standards, faculty development, and student outcomes.

Individual School Heads

Leadership for NEXUS, VITALIS, QUANTUM, AGRONOVA, and LUMEN, each accountable for programs, faculty, partners, and student success.

Transformational Leadership (Bass & Avolio)

The proposed architecture moves beyond transactional management based on reward and punishment mechanisms. It is designed around transformational leadership, where institutional structures encourage initiative, intellectual growth, and individual development.

Intellectual Stimulation

The Interdisciplinary AI Hubs operate as decentralized sandbox environments where faculty and students are structurally encouraged to challenge established industry practices, including the redesign of EdTech and FinTech frameworks through generative AI.

Individualized Consideration

Student Affairs is divided into dedicated Mentorship, Career, and Psychological Support services. This structure treats students as individuals with distinct professional, academic, and personal developmental trajectories.

Bolman & Deal's Four-Frame Model (Structural & Political)

The organizational model also reflects the structural and political dimensions of Bolman and Deal's framework, with particular attention to administrative clarity and the governance of strategic institutional resources.

The Structural Frame

The separation into Academic, Research, Commercial, and Operational pillars improves institutional efficiency, reduces administrative bottlenecks, and aligns goals with clearly defined roles and responsibilities.

The Political Frame

Placing AI Infrastructure, including HPC and GPU resources, at the executive Operations level addresses potential competition over scarce computing capacity. A centralized executive function helps neutralize internal bottlenecks and allocate resources according to institutional priorities.
